>#### caution::Learning Not to Ask Rather Than Learning How to Ask
>Many students, to varying degrees, tend to hold the following view:
>
> I ask an expert for help, the expert gives me the answer, and therefore I have learned something.
>
>But have you ever considered this? In the future, when you join a company, your manager may ask you to explore a technical solution; or when you join a research group at a university, your supervisor may ask you to investigate a new topic. You may think that there will surely be capable colleagues or senior students around to guide you. In reality, however, your colleagues have their own KPIs to meet, and senior students have their own research to work on. No one wants to be followed around and questioned all day. Sooner or later, there will be no expert available to give you the answer. How will you complete your task then?
>
>If you feel that you cannot handle it on your own, you are probably lacking <font color=red>the ability to solve problems independently</font>.
>
>Fortunately, this ability can be developed through training. The experts around you became experts because they developed their independent problem-solving skills earlier than you did. While you are still asking them a rather naive question, they may already have solved countless unusual and difficult problems. In fact, your ability is proportional to the effort you invest in solving problems independently. When an expert gives you the answer, what is demonstrated is the expert's ability, not yours. Therefore, to develop the ability to solve problems independently, it is even more important to adopt the right mindset: <font color="red">When you come here to learn, you should make every effort to solve every problem you encounter independently</font> (except, of course, for problems caused by defects in the framework code).

>#### caution:: I’m an Expert Who Really Enjoys Helping Others
>
>Take Nanjing University’s notoriously demanding PA lab as an example. We have seen this happen far too many times: at the beginning of every semester, there are always a few enthusiastic senior students from the previous cohort who patiently answer all kinds of questions from newcomers in the group chat. These newcomers do indeed feel the warmth of having those seniors shield them from the wind and rain during the grueling PA training. Yet after a month or two, those helpful seniors invariably disappear without warning, leaving the newcomers waiting helplessly in the bitter winds of PA. Meanwhile, another group of students has already developed the right mindset and skills through a month or two of struggle, grown into professionals capable of working independently, and continued moving forward under PA’s relentless pressure. We do not know why those helpful seniors suddenly disappear. Perhaps they feel the pressure of their own coursework; perhaps they grow tired of answering an endless stream of questions; perhaps they suddenly realize that the help they have been giving the newcomers is not actually helping them. Whatever the reason, those newcomers are no longer able to complete the PA training independently, even though they are about to face problems more difficult than those at the beginning of the semester.
>
>Therefore, we do not deny the sense of accomplishment you gain from helping your fellow students, but we believe there are many more serious questions to consider:
>
> > A student comes to you with a question. If that question is only a surface symptom, can you uncover the deeper problem?  
>
> > When a student cannot solve a problem without coming to you, were you truly helping them before?  
>
> >More importantly, can you take responsibility for those students’ future?
>
>If we ask, “What do you hope this student will grow into?”, I believe we all share the same goal: we hope that they will become professionals capable of working independently. At the same time, however, you must recognize a fundamental principle of learning: <font color="red">this requires long-term professional training</font>. If merely telling them the answer to a problem were enough to make them a professional, how could that be fair to the countless senior engineers who have spent years sweating on the front lines?
>
>Therefore, <font color="red">every time you directly tell them the answer or solve the problem for them, you are depriving them of an opportunity to receive professional training</font>. Conversely, if you truly want to help them, try to point out the concepts and skills they lack without interfering with their training. If they need to search for information/read manuals/read code, let them search for information/read manuals/read code. Guide them to develop their own thoughts about the problem, then try possible solutions and summarize what they have learned. This is not easy for either of you, but learning itself requires effort. If you make it too easy for them to obtain the answer, they will miss the training they should have gained from the process of solving the problem.
